Abstract

In today’s digital era, misinformation and manipulated media have become major challenges across social platforms. With the rise of artificial intelligence, deepfake images are increasingly being used to spread false information, damage reputations, and mislead audiences. To address this issue, we are developing a simple and effective solution — a Deepfake Detector that helps people verify the authenticity of digital images quickly and accurately.

The main goal of Deepfake Detector is to create a web application that can analyze uploaded images using artificial intelligence. It employs a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) model trained on real and fake image datasets to detect manipulation or forgery. The system generates a confidence score indicating whether an image is likely to be genuine or deepfake, empowering users to verify visual content before trusting or sharing it online.

The application is designed to be user-friendly and accessible from any device. With real-time image analysis and clear results, it simplifies the process of verifying authenticity for individuals, journalists, and organizations alike. Overall, Deepfake Detector aims to promote digital safety and awareness by helping users distinguish between real and AI-generated media.

INTRODUCTION

Background

With the rapid advancement of artificial intelligence, deepfake technology has made it possible to generate highly realistic but fake videos and images. These manipulations can easily deceive the human eye and are often misused for spreading misinformation, identity theft, and defamation.

The Deepfake Detection System is an AI-based solution designed to detect and analyze such manipulated media using deep learning and computer vision. The system leverages Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) trained on datasets such as FaceForensics++ to identify subtle inconsistencies and rendering artifacts that differentiate fake content from real ones.

It also includes a user-friendly web interface built with React.js and a Python (Flask/FastAPI) backend, allowing users to upload media for verification and receive instant results.

1.5 Issues / Limitations

Despite the effectiveness of the proposed deepfake detection system, certain limitations exist:

Sensitivity to Media Quality:
 Detection accuracy may decrease when processing low-quality, highly compressed, or blurry images and videos.

Advanced Manipulation Techniques:
 Highly sophisticated or previously unseen deepfake techniques may not be accurately recognized by the model.

Dataset Dependency:
 The system relies heavily on large labeled datasets (e.g., FaceForensics++). Limited diversity in training data may affect the model’s ability to generalize.

Need for Continuous Updates:
 The model requires periodic retraining to adapt to newly emerging deepfake generation methods.

Computational Requirements:
 Real-time video detection can be computationally expensive, especially on low-end or resource-constrained devices.

Probabilistic Predictions:
 The model provides confidence scores rather than absolute certainty, which may lead to ambiguous interpretations in borderline cases.

Scope Limitation:
 The current system focuses primarily on face-based manipulations and may not effectively detect non-face or audio-based deepfakes in its initial version.

CHAPTER 2 LITERATURE SURVEY

LITERATURE SURVEY

1. FaceForensics++ (2019) — Rössler et al. Built a large-scale benchmark dataset with manipulated facial images and tested multiple CNN architectures on it. Became the standard reference dataset for deepfake detection research.

2. MesoNet (2018) — Afchar et al. Designed a compact, lightweight CNN (Meso-4 / MesoInception-4) that detects facial forgeries in real time. Trades off accuracy for speed and model size.

3. FakeCatcher (2019/2020) — Ciftci et al. Detects deepfakes using biological signals — specifically blood flow patterns (rPPG) extracted from skin regions — rather than visual artifacts. Hard for generators to replicate.

4. Face X-Ray (2020) — Li et al. Identifies blending boundary artifacts left during face swapping. Generalizes across unseen manipulation types but fails on fully synthesized (non-blended) fakes.

5. Frequency Analysis for Deepfake Recognition (2020) — Frank et al. Exploits unique spectral artifacts in GAN-generated images. Works across multiple generators without retraining but may fail against diffusion-based fakes.

6. Thinking in Frequency (ECCV 2020) — Qian et al. Adds frequency-aware layers to CNNs so the network jointly learns spatial and frequency cues. Improves generalization but increases compute cost.

7. Exploiting Temporal Information (IEEE ICIP) — Guera & Delp Combines CNN (spatial features per frame) with LSTM (temporal inconsistency across frames). Simple and clean pipeline but uses older CNN backbones.

8. Deepfake Video Detection Using RNNs (IEEE AVSS) — Sabir et al. CNN extracts frame-level features, LSTM classifies at video level. Effective for video-level detection but computationally heavy.

9. CNN + LSTM for Video Forgery Detection — Zhou et al. Hybrid CNN-LSTM framework focusing on spatio-temporal forgery detection. Improves accuracy but increases memory usage and pipeline complexity.

10. Deepfake Detection Using Spatiotemporal Features (IEEE Access) — Li et al. Jointly trains CNN and LSTM to capture both spatial and temporal artifacts. Robust detection but struggles to generalize across datasets.

11. Convolutional Vision Transformer / CViT (2021) — Wodajo & Atnafu Combines CNN for local feature extraction with Vision Transformer for global attention. Achieves 91.5% accuracy and AUC of 0.91 on DFDC but limited cross-dataset generalization.

12. Deepfake Detection Using CNN-Based Architecture (2025) — Kapoor et al. Presents a complete end-to-end CNN pipeline with detailed preprocessing, architecture design, and training strategy. Practical and systematic but needs continuous updates as generation techniques evolve.

13. A Survey: Deepfake and Current Technologies (2024) — Banerjee et al. Surveys forensics, ML, multimodal, and blockchain-based detection approaches. Covers both technical and societal dimensions but real-time detection remains an open problem.

14. DeepFakes and Beyond (2020) — Tolosana et al. Classifies face manipulations into 4 types (full synthesis, identity swap, attribute manipulation, expression swap) and reviews all major datasets and benchmarks. Strong foundational reference.

15. Deepfake Generation and Detection: A Benchmark and Survey (2024) — Pei et al. Covers the full arc from GAN to diffusion-based generation and benchmarks detection methods across spatial, temporal, frequency, and hybrid domains. Most comprehensive and up-to-date survey.

Users will be provided with accessible deepfake detection through an intuitive web interface:

The system will allow users to upload images or videos without requiring technical expertise or API integration, guiding them through the upload process and presenting results with clear confidence scores.

Users will be provided with transparent detection results that explain the basis for the system's conclusions:

Users will be provided with rapid analysis capabilities for quick verification of digital content:

The system will process uploaded media efficiently, delivering detection results within seconds to minutes depending on file size, prioritizing real-time feedback.

Users will be provided with a reporting mechanism to flag detected deepfakes and contribute to community awareness:

The platform will include functionality to report suspicious media, creating a feedback loop for improving detection models and supporting broader efforts to combat misinformation.

These observations confirmed that spatial pixel features alone are insufficient for reliable deepfake detection, motivating the use of deeper feature extraction via CNNs.

ARCHITECTURAL DESIGN

ARCHITECTURAL DESIGN

3.1. Architecture Diagram

Figure 3.1  Architecture Diagram

Initial experiments using a plain baseline CNN (without attention or sequential modeling) yielded unstable performance — accuracy hovered around 50–55% with high variance across epochs (refer Figure 4.1). To address this, the model architecture was enhanced by incorporating Bi-directional LSTM layers after the CNN feature extractor, followed by an Attention mechanism. As illustrated in Figure 3.2, the final architecture processes input through a Convolutional layer and Max Pooling layer for spatial feature extraction, passes these features through an LSTM layer to capture temporal/contextual patterns, applies an Attention mechanism to focus on the most discriminative features, and finally outputs a classification via Dense and Softmax layers.

CHAPTER 5

RESULTS

RESULTS

5.1 Experimental Results

Figure 5.1 – Training Curves of Baseline CNN Model (Loss, Accuracy, AUC-ROC, F1 Score over 40 Epochs)

Figure 5.1 presents the training curves of the initial baseline CNN model trained over 40 epochs. The loss curves show minimal convergence, with validation loss remaining near 0.69 throughout training. The accuracy graph reveals that both train and validation accuracy stagnate around 50–55%, indicating the model is barely above random chance. AUC-ROC and F1 scores exhibit high variance and instability, confirming that the basic CNN architecture lacks the capacity to reliably distinguish real from fake images. These results directly motivated the architectural improvements — the addition of BiLSTM and Attention layers — which form the basis of our enhanced proposed system.

Figure 5.2 - Confusion Matrix and ROC Curve

The Fig.5.2. shows the confusion matrix where the model correctly predicts most real samples (91) but misclassifies a large number of fake samples as real (87).It detects only 13 fake instances correctly, showing poor fake detection.This indicates the model is heavily biased toward predicting real content. And on the right side, The ROC curve has an AUC of 0.55, which is close to random performance (0.5). This means the model has weak ability to distinguish between real and fake classes. Overall, the classification performance is not reliable and needs improvement.

Figure 5.3 - Classification Report

The Fig.5.3 shows the performance of the deepfake detection model for Real and Fake classes. The model performs well in identifying real instances with high recall (0.91) but struggles to detect fake ones, with a very low recall (0.13). The overall accuracy is 52%, indicating limited effectiveness. This suggests the model is biased toward predicting real content and needs improvement in detecting fake data.

CHAPTER 6

CONCLUSION

CONCLUSION

We successfully developed a Deepfake Detector, a web application designed to enhance digital security by analyzing images and detecting manipulated or AI-generated content. The application allows users to upload images for verification, enabling the system to identify visual inconsistencies and provide a confidence score indicating the likelihood of forgery. By integrating deep learning and computer vision, this system offers users a simple and reliable way to assess the authenticity of digital media. It helps individuals and organizations make informed decisions before trusting or sharing online content, thereby reducing the spread of misinformation and improving online safety. There are several techniques involved in developing such an application, including image preprocessing, feature extraction using Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), and classification based on trained datasets like FaceForensics++. Each component is crucial in delivering accurate detection results, and the combination of these techniques creates a highly functional and user-friendly tool for identifying deepfake.

Introduction

Purpose

The purpose of this document is to define the requirements for an AI-driven web platform designed to detect manipulated or AI-generated (deepfake) images. The system aims to analyze uploaded visual content and

determine its authenticity by identifying inconsistencies using deep learning and computer vision.

It provides users with a confidence score indicating the likelihood that an image is fake. Target users include social media analysts, journalists, cybersecurity professionals, and general users concerned with online misinformation.

Scope

The Minimum Viable Product (MVP) focuses on the detection and classification of manipulated or synthetic images.

Key features include:

Web-based upload interface for images and videos.

Image preprocessing and feature extraction pipeline.

Deep learning–based classification using CNN+BiLSTMmodels.

Confidence score display for authenticity verification.

Visualization of detected anomalies.

Secure result storage for auditing and history tracking.

References

Project Report on “Deepfake	Detector:	An	AI-Based	Image and Videos Forgery Detection Web Application” (core documentation).

Dataset: FaceForensics++, DeepFakeDetection, and related benchmark datasets.

IEEE Transactions on Information Forensics and Security (related research).

Preliminary Description

Proposed System Architecture

The proposed system uses a Web–Cloud architecture.

Frontend (Web Interface): Allows users to upload images and videos for analysis.

Backend (Cloud): Hosts the trained CNN model, preprocessing scripts, and the result database.

Model Layer: Performs classification and outputs a confidence score based on extracted features.

High-Level Features

Image/Video Authenticity Detection: Classify uploaded images/videos as “Real” or “Fake.”

Confidence Scoring: Quantify the likelihood of manipulation based on the model’s prediction probability.
